================================================================
WHY THIS PASS HAPPENED
================================================================
You asked me to specifically cross-check the US Tax Estimator's figures
against the data used by popular, widely-trusted US tax-calculator
websites (the kind people already use and compare against), and fix
anything that didn't line up — so the feature actually serves people
correctly, not just structurally works.

I could not run live computed results through those sites' own
interactive calculators (that needs real browser form-filling, which
this session can't do), so instead I cross-checked the underlying DATA
each of them publishes/relies on — federal and state bracket tables,
standard deductions, FICA figures, and special per-state rules — against
3-4 independent sources per figure wherever possible, specifically
targeting every number my original research had flagged as
"single-sourced" or uncertain.

================================================================
WHAT WAS WRONG, AND WHAT CHANGED
================================================================
Three real issues were found and fixed, all backed by newly-found
sources (not guesses):

1. FEDERAL HEAD OF HOUSEHOLD BRACKET — WRONG, NOW FIXED
   The original data had the 24% bracket starting at $103,700 and the
   32% bracket starting at $201,775. These were flagged as uncertain
   from day one (the one source used, kiplinger.com, had an internal
   rounding inconsistency on exactly these two numbers). Three
   independent sources now confirm the REAL 2026 thresholds are
   $105,700 and $201,750. This specifically affects anyone using Head
   of Household status (commonly single parents) with taxable income
   roughly between $103,700-$105,700 or $201,750-$201,775 — a narrow
   income band, but the estimate was genuinely wrong for people landing
   in it, and the bracket-math machinery itself needed the right number.
   Single, Married Filing Jointly, and Married Filing Separately were
   re-confirmed correct and are unchanged.

2. GEORGIA'S 2026 RATE — WRONG, NOW FIXED (this one had real dollar
   impact for every Georgia filer)
   The original data had Georgia's flat rate at 5.19% — which was
   correct for 2025, under the ORIGINAL legislated schedule of a
   further 0.1-point-per-year reduction. But a LATER law (Georgia
   H.B. 463) accelerated this, cutting 2026 straight to 4.99% instead
   (retroactive to 2026-01-01) — a 0.20-percentage-point difference.
   Confirmed directly from Georgia's own Department of Revenue 2026
   Employer's Tax Guide, which states outright: "The income tax rate
   has been reduced from a flat rate of 5.19% to a flat rate of 4.99%."
   This is exactly the kind of after-the-fact legislative change a
   single pre-season research pass can miss, which is the whole reason
   this re-validation pass was worth doing. Every Georgia filer's
   estimate was overstating their state tax by about 4% of their state
   tax bill before this fix.

3. VIRGINIA'S PERSONAL EXEMPTION — MISSING, NOW ADDED
   Virginia grants a flat personal exemption ($930 per filer, $1,860 for
   Married Filing Jointly) ON TOP OF its standard deduction — this
   wasn't modelled at all before, making every Virginia estimate a
   little too high. Now added as its own field in the data, and the
   calculation engine was extended with a small, generic
   "personalExemption" mechanism (only applies to states that define
   it — right now just Virginia; harmless and inactive for every other
   state) rather than a Virginia-specific hack.

================================================================
WHAT THIS PASS DID NOT COVER (being upfront about limits)
================================================================
  - I could not drive the actual interactive calculators on sites like
    SmartAsset, NerdWallet or PaycheckCity to get a computed number to
    diff against directly — browser automation to fill in and submit
    their forms isn't available from here. Instead I verified against
    the underlying bracket/rate data those kinds of sites are built on,
    cross-checked across multiple independent sources per figure. This
    is the same category of accuracy check a careful human reviewer
    would do, but it is not literally "the same answer as SmartAsset.com
    today."
  - This pass focused on the figures flagged as lower-confidence in the
    original research (single-sourced states/figures) plus a spot-check
    of several major, high-population states. It did not re-verify all
    51 jurisdictions line-by-line against a second source — that would
    be the next incremental step if you want it taken further.
  - The known, already-disclosed simplifications from the original
    feature (most states' deduction fallback to the federal figure,
    only NYC/Philadelphia local tax modelled, no AMT/NIIT/state credits)
    are unchanged and still clearly disclosed in the app itself.
